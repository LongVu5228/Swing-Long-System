# Qullamaggie Trading Lessons: A Synthesized Playbook

**Source:** Kristjan "Qullamaggie" Kullamägi's YouTube trading livestreams, distilled from transcripts.
**Coverage:** 2017 through December 2023 — the complete channel history, all 27 source batches processed. Part I (Sections 1-10) synthesizes 2017 through September 2020. Part II synthesizes October 2020 through December 2023, focusing on what genuinely evolved, sharpened, or emerged as new emphasis in the later period rather than repeating principles already established in Part I.
**Method:** Every transcript was read and paraphrased into discrete lessons by category, then this document synthesizes and deduplicates those notes into one coherent reference. This is a summary and paraphrase of his publicly stated views and trade narration on stream — not a verbatim transcript, and not financial advice.

---

## 1. Entries & Setups

Kullamägi consistently narrows his entire trading approach down to a small handful of repeatable chart patterns rather than a large toolkit. By 2020 he's explicit about it: he trades essentially three setups, and everything else is a variation.

**The core setup — "high tide flag" / momentum breakout.** A stock makes a large initial momentum move, then pulls back or goes sideways and finds support on a rising moving average (the 10-day for the fastest stocks, 20-day for normal swings, 50-day for slower/large-cap names). It builds a series of higher lows, the daily range tightens noticeably, and it breaks out again on above-average volume. This single pattern — surf the moving average, tighten, break out — is the one he says is worth mastering above all others, and he finds it repeating across every year and every instrument he's traded (individual stocks, leveraged ETFs, crypto proxies, commodities).

**Entry mechanics.** The trigger is almost always a break of an "opening range" — the high or low of the first 1-minute, 5-minute, or 60-minute candle after some reference point (market open, or the start of a new range). Faster timeframes (1-5 min) get you in earlier with a tighter stop but fail more often; the 60-minute candle is slower but more reliable. He buys as close to the actual breakout point as possible — chasing a stock that's already run past the trigger destroys the risk/reward, and buying in anticipation of a breakout before it happens is "dead money" that risks a reversal before the setup even confirms.

**Tightness over volatility.** The single biggest visual "tell" separating a five-star setup from a mediocre one is how tight and narrow the price range is immediately before the breakout. Loose, wide-ranging candles going into a breakout are a red flag; a stock that has stopped moving and is coiling is the one about to move violently. "If it isn't tight, it's not right."

**Parabolic shorts (and their mirror, parabolic longs).** A stock up 200-1000%+ in a short span is a short candidate — but never on day one or two of the move. He waits for day three or later, when the first real technical weakness appears (a failed opening range, a failed VWAP reclaim, a break of the rising 60-minute moving averages). Being early on these shorts has cost him some of his largest losses, including one incident where he lost roughly $1M overnight on a leveraged-ETF short that kept squeezing. The mirror setup — a beaten-down, panic-sold stock reclaiming VWAP and opening-range highs after a violent decline — is the long-side equivalent, and by his own count he sees roughly five clean short setups for every one clean long parabolic setup.

**Episodic pivots / earnings breakouts.** His favorite swing setup: a company beats earnings/revenue estimates substantially, raises guidance, gaps up double digits on volume, and trades its entire average daily volume within the first 5-15 minutes — all while breaking out of a multi-month sideways base. This combination (fundamental catalyst + technical breakout + volume confirmation) is cited repeatedly as his single highest-probability setup. He generally passes on the large majority of earnings gappers and only takes the ones with genuinely strong beats and real growth, preferring the first or second earnings pop in a name's history over the third or fourth.

**What he avoids.** Buying dips or pullbacks mid-range is explicitly called a personal weakness — he only buys confirmed breakouts, not anticipated bottoms. Frontside shorting (a stock still riding rising moving averages, not yet extended) is dangerous and has nearly hurt his account before; the two legitimate times to short are after a genuine parabolic move, or once a stock has confirmed "backside" (trading below declining averages). He rarely shorts a stock on its very first big move day, preferring to see how it digests first. And within a hot theme or sector, an individual setup doesn't need to be textbook-perfect — sector-wide momentum can carry a merely-good setup the way only a perfect setup would work in a weak market.

## 2. Chart Reading & Technical Analysis

His technical toolkit is deliberately minimal: price, volume, and a handful of moving averages — nothing more. He explicitly and repeatedly dismisses RSI, MACD, Bollinger Bands, Fibonacci retracements, and classic candlestick/chart-pattern naming (cup-and-handle, head-and-shoulders) as noise, arguing price and volume already contain whatever information those indicators are trying to extract, and that stacking more indicators just produces more contradictory signals. He estimates 90-95% of technical analysis as commonly taught is "garbage."

**The moving average framework.** On daily charts: the 10-, 20-, 50-, 100-, and 200-day simple moving averages. On the 60-minute chart: 10, 20, and sometimes 65-period exponential moving averages. Which average a stock "obeys" is itself informative — the strongest, fastest-moving stocks hold the 10-day; strong-but-slower names hold the 20-day; institutional-quality large caps use the 50-day or even weekly (10-week/20-week) averages so they aren't shaken out prematurely by daily noise. A stock "surfing" one of these averages — repeatedly touching it and bouncing, building higher lows — is read as the visible footprint of institutional accumulation, distinguishing real momentum leaders from stocks that only move on news with no natural underlying buyer.

**Relative strength is the central read.** Constantly comparing a stock's behavior to its index, sector peers, or a correlated instrument is described as core to nearly every decision. A stock that barely dips (or makes a higher low) while the broader market makes a lower low is "screaming" real strength; the inverse — a stock that can't bounce when the market bounces — is the clearest weakness signal he uses. Down days in the broader market are treated as a diagnostic gift, not something to fear, because that's when genuine relative strength (or weakness) becomes visible. Never short a stock demonstrating real relative strength, regardless of how "expensive" it looks.

**Undercut-and-reclaim.** A stock briefly dipping below a key moving average intraday and closing back above it, within an established uptrend, is read as a bullish continuation signal, not a failure — but the same undercut on a random, non-trending stock means nothing. The reverse (a failed breakdown through resistance followed by an immediate strong reclaim) is treated as a high-quality bullish tell.

**"Resistance" is mostly imaginary for real leaders.** Old volume shelves and round numbers are dismissed as meaningful resistance for genuinely strong stocks — cited examples of large caps blowing straight through supposed resistance zones repeatedly. For real leaders, only the moving-average framework matters; everything else is sold into on strength rather than pre-guessed as a ceiling.

**Leveraged and correlated instruments.** Triple-leveraged ETFs should be read off their unlevered underlying (e.g., analyze an index-leveraged fund's setup using the plain index's chart, not the leveraged fund's own distorted moving averages), since compounding/decay distorts the levered chart over time. VWAP is used mainly as an intraday pivot on extended momentum names and on the short side — failing to reclaim it is a short trigger, reclaiming and holding above it is bullish — though he admits he doesn't fully understand the underlying mechanism and simply trusts that it works.

**Market-wide diagnostics.** When several previously uncorrelated leading stocks all break their key moving averages on the same day, that's read as a much more serious warning than any single stock weakening in isolation — a distinct signal from the normal one-or-two-names-correcting rotation of a healthy bull market.

## 3. Position Sizing & Risk Management

**Risk per trade.** Typical dollar risk on any single trade is small — most commonly cited in the 0.2%-1% of account equity range, occasionally up to 1.5-2% on the highest-conviction setups. This stays mechanical regardless of account size; the process doesn't change just because the dollar amounts get larger as the account grows.

**Position concentration limits.** Never more than roughly 15-25% of account equity in a single name overnight (with some flexibility to run larger, 30-50%, intraday). A hard rule against ever putting 50%+ of an account into one stock — a single-name shock is described as something that can end a trading career outright. Sizing guidance scales loosely with account size: smaller accounts (under $100k) might concentrate in 3-5 names at up to 25% each; larger accounts spread size across more names at smaller weights, partly for liquidity reasons and partly because holding 15-20+ positions in a genuine uptrend actually reduces single-name blowup risk compared to concentrating in just a few.

**Asymmetric risk/reward is the whole game.** The recurring target is a minimum of roughly 5-10x reward relative to initial risk on longs, and 5-10x on shorts, with some citing targets as high as 10-30x on the best setups. This is the mathematical justification for a genuinely low win rate (repeatedly cited around 25-30%, sometimes lower) still producing strong overall returns — because losers are cut small and mechanically, while winners are allowed to compound far beyond the initial risk via trailing stops rather than fixed targets. A setup offering only 1:3 or 1:5 is explicitly called "not good enough" for a swing trader.

**Starter positions and scaling in.** Rather than buying full size immediately, positions are frequently built starting at a quarter-to-half of eventual size, with the remainder added only once the setup confirms with continued strength or a fresh breakout — never averaging into a loser, only ever averaging up into a winner ("pyramiding").

**Liquidity and borrow cost as sizing constraints.** Position size should scale with a stock's dollar volume — a repeated heuristic is to never be more than roughly 1% of a stock's average daily dollar volume, with a hard liquidity floor (cited variously between $5M and $20M+ average daily dollar volume) before a name is even considered tradeable at real size. On the short side, borrow availability and cost are treated as a real constraint on the trade itself, not an afterthought — he has walked away from technically "perfect" short setups purely because locate costs were prohibitive (one example cites paying over $20,000 just to locate shares for a single short), and has deliberately covered positions before a weekend specifically to avoid financing costs even when he expected the stock to keep falling.

**Margin discipline.** Leverage should be built up gradually as results improve, never jumped into all at once — margin is described as "deserved, not entitled." Different sessions cite different personal ceilings (100-130% net exposure in one telling, up to 150-200% in another during unusually strong markets), but the consistent principle is that maximum leverage is reserved for periods of demonstrated strength (both in the trader's own results and in the market's own trend), and cut back sharply, sometimes to near zero, during choppy or losing stretches.

**Order types and mechanics.** Stops are placed as market orders, never stop-limit, since a stop-limit can fail to fill entirely during a fast decline. Both "hard" stops (live resting orders) and "mental" stops (manually executed) are used — hard stops mainly reserved for when price is already near the intended exit level, to avoid unnecessary whipsaw fills from resting orders sitting too far in advance.

## 4. Trade Management (Adds, Trims, Stops)

**The signature exit framework — the "3-to-5-day rule."** By far the most consistently repeated piece of trade management across every period covered: once a breakout is confirmed and hasn't stopped the trader out, wait roughly 3 to 5 days, then sell a third to half of the position and move the stop on the remainder to breakeven. From there, the remaining shares are trailed using a moving-average close (typically the 10-day for fast movers, 20-day for slower ones) rather than an intraday touch — it's a close below the average that triggers an exit, not a wick through it. This rule flexes with market conditions: in choppy, harder-to-trade tape, trim earlier and more (half by day three); in a strongly trending market, wait longer (day five) and trim less (a third).

**Default stop placement.** For longs, the low of the entry/breakout day is used as the initial stop the large majority of the time — described in one session as "used about 90% of the time" specifically to avoid rationalizing a wider, more comfortable stop after the fact. As a position works in the trader's favor, the stop is raised incrementally, first to breakeven, then trailed progressively tighter using shorter-term references (a rising 10-day, or the 60-minute 10/20 EMA on especially extended or fast-moving names) so a large open profit isn't given back to a level appropriate for a fresh entry.

**Scaling out mechanics.** Rather than a single exit, winners are trimmed in small increments — a fixed percentage of the position, or a fixed dollar/point clip, sold repeatedly as the stock continues higher, more frequently on faster/higher-ADR names and less frequently on slower ones. This is explicitly framed as "trim and trail" being the actual operating rule, as opposed to trying to sell an exact top.

**Adding to winners vs. never averaging down.** Adds only happen on fresh confirmation of continued strength (a new range breaking further in the trend's direction), never simply because a position is already up. Averaging into a losing position is treated as one of the fastest ways to blow up an account and is explicitly and repeatedly ruled out.

**Earnings risk is handled as a hard boundary.** A recurring, near-absolute rule: never hold a full position through an earnings report, in either direction, regardless of how strong the setup looks — this is explicitly called "gambling," not trading. The exception is holding a partial position specifically when already sitting on a large profit cushion that could absorb a bad gap, and even then only with reduced size.

**Self-acknowledged weakness: selling winners too early.** This is one of the most consistently repeated personal admissions across the entire span covered — multiple named examples of stocks sold that then continued running another 30-100%+ without him. The stated fix is mechanical: trust the trailing-moving-average rule over gut instinct or fear of giving back an open profit, because in his own accounting, discretionary early selling has cost him far more upside over the years than the trailing-stop discipline has cost him in protected capital.

**Deviating from the rules backfires.** Multiple episodes are narrated where improvising — tightening a stop emotionally, or exiting early to "be smart" during a scary market dip — resulted in being shaken out of positions that then ran significantly further without him. The stated lesson, repeated almost verbatim across different years, is to follow the predefined rules rather than override them in the moment.

## 5. Profit Taking & Exit Strategy

**No fixed price targets.** Consistently rejects setting a specific price target in advance, since on a fresh breakout or new IPO with no trading history, any target is described as "a random number." Instead, the trailing moving-average stop described above functions as the entire exit mechanism — hold until the rule says to sell, not until an arbitrary number is hit.

**Profit-taking pace scales with perceived potential.** A trade expected to move a modest 15-25% is trimmed faster and in fewer stages; a trade the trader believes could double or triple is sold far more slowly, in small increments, over a much longer stretch — explicitly framed as "letting the stock tell you" how much conviction to hold rather than deciding in advance.

**Selling is harder than buying.** Stated directly and repeated across multiple periods: entries are "the easy part" of trading; disciplined, unemotional exits are the actual skill that separates profitable traders from everyone else, which is precisely why the trailing-stop system is kept as mechanical and rule-based as possible rather than left to feel or discretion.

**Defending large open profits actively.** One notable cautionary story: roughly $200,000 of unrealized profit on a position was given back in about fifteen minutes during a fast earnings reaction, specifically because the position wasn't being watched closely enough in the moment. The lesson drawn is that outsized open profits need active attention during volatile windows, not "set and forget" trailing stops alone.

## 6. Market Timing & Regime Reading

This is one of the richest and most consistent themes across the entire span — Kullamägi treats "what kind of market is this" as a constant, ongoing question that reshapes everything else about how he trades, far more than any single stock's chart.

**Two-regime framework.** Markets are repeatedly split into two broad types, borrowing language from other traders: an "easy dollar" market (a strong, persistent trend where mistakes get forgiven and nearly every reasonable setup works) versus a "hard penny" market (choppy, directionless, where only the very best setups have edge and most trades — even good-looking ones — fail to follow through). Recognizing which regime is currently active, and adjusting trade frequency and size accordingly, is treated as more important than any individual chart pattern.

**The hardest regime isn't a crash — it's chop.** A market that's obviously falling is, somewhat counterintuitively, described as easier to navigate than a market producing a stream of legitimate-looking setups that then fail to follow through, because the former makes it easy to simply sit in cash while the latter tempts continued (and increasingly costly) participation. "It's not the most active trader who wins, it's the most patient trader."

**Reading breadth and correlation.** When previously independent, uncorrelated winning stocks all start breaking down together on the same day, that's treated as a much more serious signal than any single stock's normal pullback — a hallmark of a genuine top or regime shift rather than routine rotation. Narrowing breadth (fewer sectors or names participating even while headline indices hold up) is flagged as an early caution sign well before the indices themselves show weakness. Conversely, real market crashes have historically started from price already below the 50-day/200-day moving averages — a single scary red day while a stock or index is still making new highs above rising short-term averages is, by his framing, not itself a crash signal.

**Position count and personal exposure as a sentiment gauge.** Tracking his own number of open positions and margin usage is used informally as a signal: reaching roughly 25-30+ simultaneous positions, or unusually high leverage, has more than once preceded a market pullback in his personal history — used as a self-check on excessive risk-taking rather than a formal indicator.

**Historical base rates, cited to counter emotional extremes.** References to how often equity indices have historically been in an uptrend versus not (cited figures include the Dow being up or sideways roughly 86% of the time since 1915, and average favorable stretches on the Nasdaq lasting longer than unfavorable ones) are used to push back both against "stocks only go up" complacency during euphoric periods and against excessive fear during selloffs — a 20%+ index decline is described as a fairly routine, roughly every-3-to-4-year occurrence and historically a buying opportunity, while 30-50%+ declines are rare.

**Euphoria is a warning sign, not a green light.** A market where "everything is straight up" with no pullbacks, or where low-quality/previously-lagging stocks (pump names, weak cyclicals) suddenly start outperforming the prior leaders, is treated as a late-cycle, high-risk signal — repeatedly described as the most dangerous time to be aggressively long, precisely because it feels like the easiest, most rewarding environment. His own rising personal excitement or euphoria about the market is explicitly used as a contrarian signal to start hedging or reducing exposure ("one foot out the door").

**Sitting out is a valid, even superior, strategy.** During choppy or unclear stretches, deliberately doing less — fewer trades, smaller size, or no trades at all — is repeatedly framed not as passivity but as a specific skill, one that most losing traders fail to exercise because it's psychologically uncomfortable to do nothing while others are (apparently) making money.

## 7. Watchlist & Stock Selection Criteria

**Liquidity and volatility filters come before anything else.** Two numbers gate every other consideration: Average Daily Range (ADR%, a measure of how much a stock typically moves per day) and average dollar volume. Minimum ADR thresholds cited range from roughly 4% up to 6-7%+ depending on the period and account size discussed; low-ADR, low-volatility "blue chip" stocks (examples repeatedly cited include Microsoft, Apple, Visa, Costco) are explicitly ruled out as "not trading stocks" regardless of how good their charts look, because they simply can't move enough, fast enough, to be worth the capital. Minimum dollar-volume floors are cited anywhere from roughly $5M to $60M+ per day depending on account size and period, with the general principle that the floor should scale with how much capital needs to be deployed.

**Momentum and relative strength, not value.** Scans are consistently built around price momentum — biggest percentage gainers over rolling 1-, 3-, 6-, 12-, and sometimes 18-24 month lookback windows, filtered by the liquidity criteria above and ranked by relative strength. Fundamental value screens (cheap valuation multiples, dividend yield, etc.) are never mentioned as a selection criterion; growth (particularly accelerating revenue growth, more so than current profitability or EPS) is used only as secondary supporting "fuel" for a technical setup already in place, never as a standalone reason to buy.

**Institutional-quality names vs. pure pump stocks.** A recurring distinction is drawn between higher-priced, liquid "institutional quality" momentum leaders — the kind that smoothly obey moving averages because real funds are accumulating them — versus thin, illiquid, purely retail-driven "pump" stocks with no natural institutional buyer, which need constant news flow to keep moving and behave far less predictably. Both categories get traded, but with very different size and risk treatment; some sessions mention keeping a separate, small, deliberately isolated account specifically for speculative pump-stock trades so losses there can't touch the main portfolio.

**Sector and theme awareness.** When a sector or theme is running (biotech, EV/SPACs, Chinese internet stocks, vaccine names, commodities, whatever is dominant in a given market cycle), the preference is to trade the single fastest, most liquid name within that theme rather than every look-alike, and sector-wide participation (several related names breaking out together) is treated as a stronger, more reliable signal than an isolated single-stock mover. Each market cycle is noted as having had its own dominant growth theme historically (commodities in the mid-2000s, biotech in the mid-2010s, software/internet more recently), and the discipline is to keep rotating attention to wherever the fastest current momentum actually is, rather than staying loyal to a theme that has stopped working.

**What gets filtered out entirely.** Random illiquid microcaps and OTC "pump" names without an identifiable technical or earnings catalyst are passed on even when the chart looks superficially decent, on the grounds that there's no real institutional participation and therefore no durable edge. Binary biotech catalysts (FDA approval dates, trial data) are generally avoided as position risk because the outcome is a coin-flip regardless of chart quality. Insider buying/selling and short-seller "hit piece" reports (Citron, Muddy Waters, etc.) are both treated as largely irrelevant to the decision — the stated approach is to follow price action itself rather than any secondary informational signal.

## 8. Psychology & Mindset

**Emotion is not the enemy — losing the argument with it is.** A recurring, explicit pushback against generic "trade with no emotion" advice: emotions like fear, FOMO, frustration, and greed are treated as inevitable and even useful, as long as they're channeled into following a predefined process rather than suppressed or denied. Anyone claiming total emotional detachment while trading is described bluntly as "full of it."

**Discipline over being right.** "Love your stops, not your dogs" — a line credited to another trader (Dan Sanger) and repeated as a core operating principle: prioritizing mechanical rule-following over the emotional urge to be proven correct on any individual trade thesis. A stop-out is deliberately reframed as the market neutrally saying "not ready yet," not a personal failure or an attack — a framing explicitly adopted to make it psychologically easier to take stops without hesitation.

**Patience as the central skill.** Across nearly every period, patience — not activity — is named as the actual differentiator between traders who succeed and those who don't. Trading is described as roughly 90-95% waiting and boredom; getting visibly excited about a trade is treated as a personal warning sign to size down, not a green light to size up. Real intuition, as opposed to noise masquerading as a gut feeling, is only considered reliable after roughly five years and thousands of hours of screen time.

**Losses are the cost of doing business, not a personal failing.** A repeated ratio cited from his own results: roughly $800,000-900,000 in cumulative losses for every $1,000,000 in gains is described as a completely normal cost structure for this style of trading — big winners require absorbing frequent, deliberately small losses along the way. A nine-month sideways drawdown, occurring even after he was already a consistently profitable trader, is offered as evidence that drawdowns are "a feature, not a bug" of the process — something to minimize through position sizing, never something a good process eliminates entirely.

**Reviewing and owning mistakes publicly.** Monthly (and sometimes trade-by-trade) reviews specifically cataloging mistakes are described as a deliberate practice, with the explicit estimate that eliminating his own top recurring errors in any given month could plausibly increase that month's returns by 50-100%. Real-time mistakes — early stop-outs, oversized earnings holds, fat-fingered orders — are narrated on stream as teaching material rather than edited out or hidden.

**Complacency after success is a specific, named risk.** Some of his largest historical losses are described as occurring directly after his best stretches — easy, forgiving markets and hot streaks are said to breed the oversized risk-taking and sloppy execution that eventually produce a large loss. Deliberately cutting size immediately after a big win or a big accidental loss (to avoid both complacency and revenge trading) is mentioned as an explicit countermeasure.

**Real skill isn't transferable secondhand.** Following another trader's specific stock picks is explicitly said to never produce consistent profitability on its own — ideas can be borrowed, but the actual skill (pattern recognition built from studying thousands of historical charts, refined through the trader's own losses) has to be built individually and can't be shortcut by copying someone else's trades. Relatedly, verifying any rule (which moving average actually works for a given kind of stock, for instance) against a large personal sample of historical charts, rather than taking any mentor's word for it, is presented as how genuine conviction — the kind that survives a drawdown without being abandoned — actually gets built.

## 9. Common Mistakes / What Not To Do

Synthesizing the most frequently repeated warnings across the full span:

1. **Getting stubborn.** Refusing to cut a loss quickly, or clinging to a fixed thesis after price has proven it wrong, is cited as his single most frequently named recurring flaw, responsible for some of his largest individual losses (including one episode costing roughly $700,000, about 20% of account equity at the time).
2. **Chasing.** Buying a stock after it's already run well past its actual breakout/base trigger — "the second day after it's already up a bunch" — repeatedly destroys the risk/reward of an otherwise good idea.
3. **Being early on shorts.** Trying to call an exact top on a strong or parabolic stock, rather than waiting for confirmed technical weakness, is named across multiple years as a low-probability habit that produces avoidable losses; the correct approach is to short *after* weakness is confirmed, not to anticipate it.
4. **Holding through earnings without a plan.** Doing so purely out of habit, conviction, or because a setup "looks too good to sell" is explicitly labeled gambling rather than trading, distinct from deliberately holding a partial position with a profit cushion.
5. **Overriding predefined rules "to be smart" in the moment.** Multiple narrated episodes show discretionary deviation from the trailing-stop/exit system producing worse outcomes than simply following the mechanical rule would have.
6. **Sizing shorts too aggressively in cheap stocks.** Because a low-priced stock can double or triple even after already doubling, the math of percentage moves works structurally against an oversized short position.
7. **Trading without an identifiable, repeatable edge.** Taking positions in random tickers with no defined setup is called a direct path to blowing up an account, regardless of how good any individual story sounds.
8. **Ignoring liquidity and borrow constraints.** Committing size to illiquid names (long or short) despite unreliable fills, or ignoring the cost/availability of a short locate before entering, has produced avoidable, sometimes large, losses.
9. **Complacency and fat-fingered execution during fast, high-volume periods.** Multiple costly order errors are attributed directly to trading too much size across too many simultaneously moving positions without enough attention on execution.
10. **Options and CFDs.** Both are avoided almost entirely and described in strongly negative terms (a "sucker's game" driven by lottery-ticket psychology, and "poison" leverage, respectively) — the stated position is that a trader shouldn't touch either until already consistently profitable trading straight equities, if ever.
11. **Believing in market manipulation narratives.** Blaming "stop hunting" by market makers, or short-seller conspiracy theories, for a losing trade is dismissed outright — losses are attributed to normal algorithmic price movement and the trader's own process, not manipulation.

## 10. Other Notable Lessons

**Reading list.** *Reminiscences of a Stock Operator* (the Jesse Livermore biography) is named repeatedly as his all-time favorite trading book, along with William O'Neil's *How to Make Money in Stocks* (the source of the CAN SLIM framework — combining price momentum with earnings momentum), the *Market Wizards* series, and Michael Covel's *Trend Following*. Several specific Livermore rules are endorsed near-verbatim on stream: don't trade every day, distrust your own opinion until price confirms it, never let a trade quietly become an "investment" by ignoring your stop, and never average into a loser.

**The core skill-building method.** Building a large personal database of historical winning (and losing) stock chart setups — cited at "thousands" of annotated examples accumulated over many years in a note-taking tool (Evernote is mentioned specifically) — is described as the single most valuable exercise for developing real pattern recognition, more useful than any course, mentor, or book alone. This is framed as requiring at least 1,000+ hours of dedicated study before a trader can expect to develop genuine, tested setups and the conviction to hold them through a drawdown.

**Day trading vs. swing/position trading.** Day trading is consistently described as a tool for growing a small account quickly, not a long-term destination — as the account grows into the high six or seven figures and beyond, day-trading strategies stop scaling and the natural shift is toward swing and position trading, which captures full-sized multi-week-to-multi-month moves instead of small intraday scalps. The transition is described as something that took over a year to make deliberately, not something to rush.

**Markets are not zero-sum.** Unlike a poker table, aggregate equity returns are framed as driven by genuine value creation — companies converting capital and assets into something more valuable over time and distributing free cash flow via buybacks and dividends — which is offered as part of the underlying rationale for a fundamentally bullish long-term bias even amid frequent short-term trading in both directions.

**News, politics, and macro are treated as noise.** A recurring, sometimes blunt refrain across nearly every year covered: politics, Fed policy, and general macro commentary are explicitly said to have no predictive value for his process, and time spent on them is framed as wasted. Price action and the specific setups on the screen are what matter; narratives and opinions — his own included — are subordinate to what price is actually doing.

**Tools.** The consistent platform stack through this period is TC2000 (used for scanning and charting, not execution) paired with a fast, stable execution platform (Sterling Trader Pro and/or Interactive Brokers), with a preference for speed, simplicity, and reliability over feature count — slower or buggier platforms (ThinkorSwim is criticized specifically) are avoided even when they offer more built-in tools.

# Part II: October 2020 – December 2023

The core framework in Part I — moving-average trailing stops, small-defined-risk/large-asymmetric-reward, patience through chop, relentless self-critique of early selling — stays intact through this whole later period. What follows is what actually changed, sharpened, or got more explicit emphasis, rather than restating principles already covered above.

## 11. The Shift Toward Episodic Pivots

The single biggest evolution across this period is a visible shift in relative emphasis from "buy any tight breakout" toward the **episodic pivot (EP)** — a large-volume gap on a genuine earnings/news surprise — becoming the dominant, most-trusted setup, especially from 2021 onward. By the 2022 bear market and into 2023, he states plainly that ordinary technical breakouts had become mostly unreliable and stopped working well except during confirmed, broad-based uptrends; EPs, by contrast, kept working because they're driven by a real fundamental surprise rather than pure technical pattern-following, which holds up better when the market itself is choppy or declining.

The EP recipe gets progressively more precise through this period: a stock gapping at least high-single-digits to low-double-digits percent (scaled to the stock's own normal volatility, not a fixed number) on a genuine surprise, trading a large multiple of its normal volume in the first few minutes, and — critically, a refinement that sharpens over time — gapping *over* its declining moving averages into "clean air" rather than gapping into a zone where overhead resistance is likely to cap the move. An EP that gaps up but remains trapped below still-declining longer-term averages is treated as structurally weaker and more likely to fail than one that clears all overhead resistance outright. He's also explicit that most gap-ups are not real EPs — a large majority of earnings gappers are passed on, and the entry window on the genuine ones is usually just the first several minutes of trading, not something to leisurely evaluate later in the day.

By 2023, he describes his own trading day as built almost entirely around this one setup: roughly one hour of total daily engagement, split between pre-market/after-hours EP scanning and the first 30-45 minutes of regular trading, which by his own estimate captures 70-80% of his actual trading activity for the day. This is a marked contraction from the more varied, longer-session trading style described in the earlier years.

## 12. New and Refined Setup Vocabulary

Several concepts get named, formalized, or refined during this period that were present only informally (or not at all) earlier:

- **Pocket pivots** — a breakout occurring *inside* an existing base or range, rather than off a clean multi-week consolidation into new territory; treated as a distinct, slightly lower-conviction variant of the standard breakout, more common on larger/slower stocks.
- **VCP (volatility contraction pattern)** — he explicitly avoids the jargon, simply calling it "tightening range with higher lows," but acknowledges it as the same concept popularized elsewhere (Minervini) under that name.
- **Mean-reversion setups** get much more explicit articulation as their own category, separate from momentum breakouts: a stock that has moved sharply for several consecutive days (in either direction) and shows the first real reversal signal is treated as a distinct, faster, higher-frequency setup — particularly favored for day trading — from the slower momentum-breakout swing trades.
- **"Frontside" vs. "backside"** becomes a consistently used shorthand: frontside means a stock is still finding support on rising moving averages (dangerous to short); backside means those same averages have flipped to resistance, confirming a genuine trend change and making it safe to initiate a short. This distinction, present in earlier years, becomes one of the most frequently repeated single concepts in the 2020-2021 material specifically.
- **"Tennis ball action"** (a term he credits to Mark Minervini) — a stock that gets pushed down and immediately bounces back, like a tennis ball hitting the ground — is adopted as shorthand for visible relative strength, used repeatedly from 2021 onward.

## 13. Market Regime Case Studies: Meme-Stock Mania and the 2022 Bear Market

Two distinct, extended regime episodes in this period produce an unusually large amount of explicit commentary and serve almost as case studies in his own framework:

**The GameStop/AMC/meme-stock mania (January-February 2021).** He treats this period consistently as a cautionary tale about chasing purely social-media-driven momentum with no technical setup underneath it — explicitly warning, in real time, against buying a stock purely because it's already up thousands of percent and "everyone else" is excited, which he identifies as the core mistake behind most retail losses during the mania. He also uses the period to reinforce a rule about position risk concentration: even a small portfolio allocation (cited as low as 1-3%) to a name that is later forced into a violent short squeeze can be enough to wreck an entire year's performance for anyone positioned against it, which he ties directly to well-known fund losses from that period. His own approach during the mania was to treat it as tradeable on technicals like anything else (never shorting on day one of a parabolic move, waiting for confirmed backside weakness) while explicitly not treating the "why" behind the move — genuine short squeeze vs. any other catalyst — as relevant to execution.

**The 2022 bear market.** This is the most sustained drawdown-regime commentary in the entire span. Key reinforced and new points: bear markets can run for a long time and contain multiple sharp multi-month rally phases within them, so a two-month decline shouldn't be mistaken for the whole cycle (he references 2000-2003 and 2007-2009 as the closest historical shape comparisons). "Easy money" windows still occur even within an overall bear-market year — usually once or twice — and the skill is being ready to size up hard when one appears rather than staying defensive the whole year. In a highly correlated, choppy bear-market tape, individual stock-picking becomes much harder and he leans more heavily on leveraged/sector ETFs, since single-stock idiosyncrasies get swamped by the broader index correlation anyway. During the bear market's deepest, fastest sell-offs, the most beaten-down names tend to lead the recovery's first leg; during shallower pullbacks within an ongoing uptrend, it's instead the names already showing the most relative strength that lead once pressure lifts — a useful, explicitly stated distinction between "recovery from a crash" leadership and "pullback within a trend" leadership. He also introduces a simple earnings-reaction heuristic from this period: what matters is not whether a report beat or missed, but whether the result was better or worse than the market's *feared* expectation — the market rewarding an outright bad number is treated as a meaningful bullish regime-change tell.

## 14. Sell-Rule Discipline: An Escalating Self-Critique

"I sell winners too early" is present in Part I, but across the 2020-2023 material this becomes by far the single most repeated self-criticism in the entire corpus — mentioned in nearly every batch from this period, often with specific dollar figures and named tickers attached (missed continuations worth tens to hundreds of thousands of dollars, sometimes describing a position that ran another 50-150%+ after an early, discretionary exit). Several distinct refinements to the exit framework emerge directly from this recurring self-critique:

- On stocks that have moved extremely fast (100%+ in a handful of days), the standard daily moving-average trail is explicitly called insufficient — the stock will give back too much before triggering a daily close-below signal — and the fix is either an intraday trailing reference (a 60-minute or even faster moving average) or actively selling into strength in small increments rather than waiting for any trailing signal at all.
- A repeated, almost mantra-like line from this period: "you cannot outsmart the moving average" — every attempt to override the mechanical trailing-stop system using discretion or a "feels extended" gut call is described, across multiple separate years, as having cost more money on net than it protected.
- He also names the flip side explicitly for the first time in this period: overriding sell rules isn't purely a mistake to be engineered away — on rare, exceptional positions he acknowledges deliberately keeping a runner past where the mechanical rule would have exited, accepting that this means being "wrong" by his own rule on some individual trades in exchange for capturing rare outlier winners. The tension between these two ideas (trust the rule vs. occasionally override it for a true outlier) is presented as unresolved and genuinely difficult, not a solved problem.

## 15. Position Sizing and Risk at a Larger Scale

As his account size grew through this period, several sizing and risk practices get more explicit articulation:

- A repeated informal warning signal: hitting roughly 25-30 simultaneous open positions has, in his own trading history, preceded a market pullback multiple separate times — not treated as causal, but used consistently as a self-check to start trimming and de-risking.
- Margin is framed even more explicitly as something that has to be "deserved" through a demonstrated run of good results, not something to use simply because it's available — and the same margin used well in a strongly trending market becomes actively dangerous the moment conditions shift, requiring unusually fast exposure cuts at the first sign of trouble.
- He introduces more explicit reference points for risk sizing at scale, including citing half-Kelly criterion sizing as a reasonable ceiling when a trader can genuinely estimate their own edge, and capping overall portfolio leverage around 1.5x even at large account size.
- The concept of a **"small account edge"** gets named and developed explicitly in this period: a small account can concentrate meaningfully in illiquid, high-ADR micro- and small-cap stocks capable of doubling, tripling, or more in days — moves a large account simply cannot access at meaningful size without moving the stock against itself. This is framed as a genuine structural advantage smaller traders have over him, not just a limitation to work around, and he recommends smaller accounts lean into it deliberately (concentrated position counts, aggressive sizing on the highest-conviction setups) rather than trying to imitate the diversification appropriate for a much larger account.
- He repeatedly studies and references historical trading blowups from this period specifically to reverse-engineer recurring failure patterns — citing Archegos/Bill Hwang, Long-Term Capital Management, and cases of over-concentrated margin positions — consistently attributing these to some combination of excessive leverage, absent exit rules, and over-concentration in a single name or thesis, rather than to any lack of intelligence or market insight on the part of the people involved.

## 16. Psychology: From Discretion to "Extreme Ownership"

The psychology material in this period shifts noticeably from earlier, more introspective framing toward a harder, more accountability-focused stance. A recurring formulation from this stretch: struggling traders' problems are attributed overwhelmingly to not yet having a real, sufficiently-studied edge — not to some separate, fixable "trading psychology" deficiency that can be addressed through coaching or mindset work in isolation from the underlying skill. Paid trading psychologists and generic mindset coaching are treated skeptically for the same reason; discipline is described as something that emerges naturally once a trader has deeply internalized a specific setup's actual statistics (when it works, when it fails), rather than a separate character trait to be trained.

Other recurring threads specific to this period: every loss is framed as the trader's own responsibility, explicitly rejecting explanations involving market maker "manipulation" or algorithmic stop-hunting as a cop-out. Following other traders' specific picks — even ones with large, real followings — is repeatedly warned against, since real, durable trading conviction can only be built by personally verifying a rule against a large sample of historical charts, not borrowed secondhand. He's also more explicit in this period that emotional control cannot be taught remotely through books or streams — it's built only through direct, personal, often painful experience over years, and anyone claiming to have fully solved it is treated with suspicion.

## 17. Tools, Practicalities, and Career Reflections

Some concrete operational details specific to this later period, offered as practical color rather than core methodology:

- Platform preferences shift somewhat — TradingView is adopted and praised for interface and community despite having weaker scanning tools than TC2000, while ThinkorSwim is criticized further and MarketSmith is criticized for slow data updates by 2023.
- He describes deliberately shifting a larger share of his trading into a tax-advantaged account structure and reducing his short-selling activity over time, citing both tax efficiency and reduced screen time as motivations — framing shorting as a strategy that scales poorly for a professional manager anyway, since a short's maximum gain is capped at 100% while a long's upside is theoretically unlimited.
- He states a blanket rule against ever sending money to anyone who contacts him unsolicited, offered as general anti-scam advice given how frequently his public profile attracts impersonation and fraud attempts.
- Reflecting on his own trajectory, he frames the transition from day trading to swing/position trading as something that happens naturally as an account outgrows what day trading can scale to — day trading remains useful for growing a small account quickly, or for traders who specifically want frequent, quick feedback, but stops being the highest-value use of time once real size is achieved.
